# Turn Harvest's correctness lead into leverage

By October 2026 durable execution has become a commodity. The basic primitives (durable steps, timers, waits for human input, schedules, "model call = activity") ship in Temporal, Restate, DBOS, Inngest, Vercel, Cloudflare and AWS Lambda. Competition has moved to three unsolved problems. The first is **safely upgrading code under long-lived runs**. The second is **the cost of replaying ever-growing histories**, which AI-agent loops make worse. The third is **agent-specific needs** such as cost control, forking and protocol-native long tasks. The research literature runs ahead of every shipping engine on speculation, workflow-level atomicity and machine-checked semantics. It is weakest exactly where practitioners hurt most: proving that a code change is compatible with in-flight runs. Autumn Harvest (v0.7.0, HEAD `301ea95`) is unusually well placed for that gap. It already has more determinism and assurance machinery than any peer: compile-time guardrails, a MIR taint verifier, TLA+ models, deterministic simulation at 4M seeds a night, and a version-gate retirement guard. It also co-locates workflow state with application data in Postgres. Its weaknesses are just as concrete. It trails Temporal by **1.5–2x even in its best mode**, and by 7.91x at its default claim path under backlog. Its agent layer is thin. Its newest hot-path fixes have never been load-tested. The highest-value R&D therefore turns the verification stack into user-facing products. In ranked order, the bets are **per-run upgrade-compatibility verdicts**, an **agent cost ledger with budgets and cross-run caching**, **non-destructive fork plus replay-as-evaluation**, wider assurance coverage, and a **spec-compliant MCP Tasks adapter**. A claim-path rebuild with a published post-fix benchmark is the prerequisite for all of them. The longer-horizon bets are making workflows statically analyzable, contention-adaptive atomicity in the style of CIDR 2026's "AC/DC", and speculative persistence.

## The market converged on primitives and now competes on replay, versioning and agents

Engines split into three execution families. **Deterministic event-sourced replay** re-runs a workflow function against its history: Temporal, Cadence, Azure Durable Functions, Vercel Workflow and AWS Lambda durable functions. **Journaled step memoization** checkpoints only step results: DBOS, Inngest, Restate, Absurd and Cloudflare Workflows. **Process or VM state capture** covers Trigger.dev's CRIU checkpoints ([indepth.dev](https://indepth.dev/posts/1020/en/how-trigger-dev-checkpoints-containers)) and Golem's WASM oplog ([Golem](https://learn.golem.cloud/concepts)). The families are converging from opposite ends. Replay vendors are cutting replay cost: Inngest's checkpointing reports **about 50% faster execution** ([Inngest](https://www.inngest.com/blog/introducing-checkpointing)), and Restate 1.6 restarts an invocation from a journal prefix ([Restate 1.6](https://github.com/restatedev/restate/blob/main/release-notes/v1.6.0.md)). Both camps now pin runs to the code version that started them.

Hyperscalers turned the basic primitive into a feature of compute. AWS Lambda durable functions went GA in December 2025 at **$8 per million durable operations** ([AWS pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/lambda/pricing/)). Cloudflare began per-step billing in August 2026, so even a `sleep` is now a billed step ([Cloudflare community](https://community.cloudflare.com/t/workflows-workflows-pricing-adds-per-step-billing-step-and-storage-billing-to-start-no-earlier-than-august-10-2026/938295)). Vercel Workflow went GA in April 2026 ([Vercel docs](https://vercel.com/docs/workflow)). Capital went to the leader. Temporal raised **$550M at a $12.55B valuation** in September 2026, with ARR above $250M and **1.9 trillion billable actions in August, up 350% year on year** ([Temporal](https://temporal.io/blog/temporal-raises-usd550m-series-e-at-usd12-55b-valuation-ai)). Restate raised a $20M Series A on an agent-infrastructure pitch ([tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2026/09/30/berlin-based-restate-raises-20m/)). Every 2025–2026 launch leans toward AI. That growth explains why vendors now race on streaming, payload size and sandboxes instead of basic durability.

Temporal's Replay 2026 list shows where the frontier now sits ([Temporal Replay 2026](https://temporal.io/blog/replay-2026-product-announcements)). It shipped Worker Versioning, Priority & Fairness, and multi-region replication with a 20-minute RTO as GA. It previewed Workflow Streams for LLM tokens, External Payload Storage for S3 claim-check, Standalone Activities and Serverless Workers. Nexus cross-namespace calls are GA in Python, and Principal Attribution (a non-spoofable identity in history) is pre-release. One item on that list matters directly to Harvest's positioning: a **Temporal Rust SDK entered public preview**. In the same year DBOS shipped a **Rust SDK v0.5 aimed at embedded use** ([DBOS](https://www.dbos.dev/blog/whats-new-in-dbos-september-2026)). "Rust-native durable workflows on Postgres" is therefore no longer an empty niche.

Limits and user complaints locate the open problems. Per-run ceilings cluster at **25K–51K events**: Temporal Cloud stops at 51,200 events or 50 MB ([Temporal limits](https://docs.temporal.io/cloud/limits)). Vercel caps replay at 240 s and warns that runs past 2,000 events replay slowly ([Vercel limits](https://vercel.com/docs/workflows/pricing)). Long agent loops still have to be chunked. Practitioners name the determinism tax and replay-versus-deploy breakage as the "nastiest production failure" ([iotdigitaltwinplm](https://iotdigitaltwinplm.com/durable-execution-architecture-temporal-restate-dbos-2026/)). An HN thread repeats that steps remain at-least-once, so idempotency is still the user's job, and praises DBOS because it "Just Uses Postgres" ([HN 46245238](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46245238)). By late 2026 the table stakes are durable steps, timers, HITL waits, cron, pause/resume/restart, metrics, some form of version pinning, concurrency limits, fairness, and AI SDK integrations with streaming. The remaining differentiators sit in data governance (per-tenant keys, erasure, audit identity), cross-service durable calls, fork or restart-from-step, and embedded deployment.

### Agents, WASM and edge reshape what "durable" must mean

The agent integration pattern is now uniform. Each LLM call and tool call becomes a journaled step, and the loop replays from the journal. OpenAI even made its Agents SDK `Runner` an abstract class so that Temporal could supply a durable runner ([Temporal](https://temporal.io/blog/announcing-openai-agents-sdk-integration)). Because the pattern is a commodity, differentiation lies in what it does badly. **No engine records tokens, model or cost per step**. Temporal's Billing API is account-level ([Temporal Replay 2026](https://temporal.io/blog/replay-2026-product-announcements)). **Memoization works only within one run's replay**; nobody caches LLM outputs across runs. **Fork-from-step exists only in snapshot frameworks**: LangGraph resumes from a checkpoint and "produces a new fork in history" ([LangChain docs](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/use-time-travel)). No journal engine markets a fork tree or replay-based evaluation. Dapr's vendor Diagrid sums up the critique of agent frameworks as "checkpointing is a storage operation, not a reliability guarantee" ([Diagrid](https://www.diagrid.io/blog/still-not-durable-how-microsoft-agent-framework-and-strands-agents-repeat-the-same-mistake)). The statement comes from a competitor, but it describes a gap a real engine can fill.

The protocol layer opened a concrete slot. MCP's Tasks capability became the official extension `io.modelcontextprotocol/tasks` in the 2026-07-28 spec. It has `working`, `input_required`, `completed`, `failed` and `cancelled` states, TTL handles and non-blocking elicitation. The summary of the spec **says nothing about server-side durability, retries or exactly-once creation** ([AAIF](https://aaif.io/blog/handle-long-running-mcp-tool-calls-with-the-mcp-tasks-extension)). Users still file issues because 60 s synchronous tool timeouts block long tools ([claude-code #76571](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/76571)). No first-party "workflow as MCP Task server" adapter from Temporal, Restate, DBOS or Inngest turned up.

WASM's offer is transparent journaling of every host call, a deterministic sandbox, suspend-to-zero, and a natural point to enforce capabilities. Golem now sells itself as "the durable agent runtime", with capability "cards" and per-agent sandboxes ([Golem](https://golem.cloud/blog/the-rise-of-the-agent-runtime/)). It still tracks exactly-once database effects as an open issue ([golem #1514](https://github.com/golemcloud/golem/issues/1514)). On the edge, per-operation billing makes step granularity a cost decision. Billed replay compute on Lambda turns replay cost from a hidden tax into an invoice line.

## Research leads production on speculation, atomicity and formal semantics, but not on upgrades

Academic work treats replay-based durable execution as functionally solved. It is open in performance and semantics. The strongest performance signal comes from **speculative persistence**. Netherite's speculative communication, DARQ's composable resilient steps and libDSE all let code run ahead of the log and repair state after a crash. libDSE reports **up to 10x lower end-to-end latency** against Temporal and Durable Functions baselines ([libDSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13314); [Netherite](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol15/p1591-burckhardt.pdf)). Halfmoon proves that logging only reads or only writes is enough for exactly-once. It cuts logging overhead by **1.5–4.0x** against Boki ([Halfmoon](https://doi.org/10.1145/3600006.3613154)). No mainstream engine exposes either idea as a user-facing guarantee.

On semantics, Stonebraker and colleagues argue at CIDR 2026 that durability is necessary but not sufficient. They propose "AC/DC" (atomic, consistent, durable, correct) for whole workflows. Their prototype offers per-step physical backout, one Postgres transaction with a savepoint per step, as an alternative to sagas. The trade-off is sharp. Physical backout is **up to 1.8x faster under low contention**. With long-running steps, **saga goodput is 15.9 against 2.0** for backout, and P90 latency is 1,100 ms against 24,554 ms ([CIDR 2026](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p9-stonebraker.pdf)). The same paper lists hybrid backout, escrow for commutative operations, and formal saga isolation levels as future work, and calls workflow consistency "not-yet-implemented". Only an engine that shares a database with the application can build these ideas. DBOS and Harvest are the obvious candidates.

Formal verification of engines is real but sparse. Durable Functions is the only orchestration engine with a published formal semantics and equivalence proof ([OOPSLA 2021](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2021/10/DF-Semantics-With-Proofs.pdf)). Resonate sets the public bar for engine testing. It combines an executable Lean 4 spec covering 18 of 21 client handlers, differential testing against an oracle, DST of its SDK, and trace-checking of production traffic. It admits that **63 of 95 properties lack full proof** ([Resonate](https://docs.resonatehq.io/evaluate/how-resonate-is-tested)). The research found **no public TLA+ or P model of Temporal or Cadence, and no Jepsen-style analysis of any workflow engine**. That absence comes from a few searches, not an exhaustive survey. Trace validation of implementation logs against TLA+ ([arXiv 2404.16075](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16075)) offers a cheap bridge between a spec and a running engine.

Upgrades are the most practically important area and the thinnest in the literature. Industry tooling stops at three dials: pin the run (Temporal PINNED, Restate, Vercel, DBOS source hashes), branch in code (patch markers, Azure `context.Version`), or migrate state explicitly (Golem snapshots) ([Temporal](https://temporal.io/blog/ga-worker-versioning-public-preview-upgrade-on-continue-as-new); [Golem](https://learn.golem.cloud/how-to-guides/common/golem-update-running-agents)). Temporal's GA announcement concedes that patching brings "real code complexity and cognitive overhead". It offers no detector for incompatible changes. The first formal model of upgrade risk appeared in July 2026. It names three failure modes: determinism violation, rehydration failure (old payloads no longer fit new types) and behavioral drift (replay succeeds but future steps change). It proves that a zero backward-risk verdict guarantees safe rehydration, but it scores risk from telemetry and names no engine ([Maraschi and Collina](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13617)). Its authors run a vendor, Platformatic. Replay testing only catches the first failure mode, and it stalls on encrypted or PII-bearing production histories ([Tenzer](https://keithtenzer.com/temporal/Altering_Space-Time_Continuum_Testing_for_Determinism/)).

Agent durability is the newest strand, almost entirely 2026 preprints. Meta's LogAct records intended actions in a shared log before it executes them. Pluggable voters then approve or block each action ([LogAct](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.07988v1)). A single-author paper model-checks resume semantics. It reports that LangGraph 1.2.9 "durably records a second resume value and never consults it" ([Resume Means Resume](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03836)). Two more papers show that retry, resume and rollback can repeat a user's authorization under fresh token IDs, and that checkpoint rollback is an attack surface ([arXiv 2608.01710](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01710v1); [arXiv 2608.29381](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.29381)). AstronOS found that handing a fresh model session versioned authoritative state passed **14 of 15 runs, against 2 of 15 for full-history replay** ([AstronOS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16381)). That is a small benchmark, but it questions pure replay as an agent recovery strategy. Treat all of these as signals of direction, not settled results.

### Postgres engines hit the same three walls, and each has a known workaround

The storage evidence agrees on where Postgres-backed engines saturate. DBOS reached **43K no-op workflows/s on a 96-vCPU RDS node**, and one process flushing the WAL was the limit. Its single queue capped at **12.1K/s from head-of-queue lock contention**, rising to **30.6K/s with partitioned queues** ([DBOS benchmark](https://www.dbos.dev/blog/benchmarking-workflow-execution-scalability-on-postgres)). LISTEN/NOTIFY serializes commits and disables group commit. Buffered, batched NOTIFY took DBOS from 2.9K to 60K writes/s at 15–100 ms latency ([DBOS](https://dbos.dev/blog/postgres-listen-notify-scalability)). Hatchet abandoned plain `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` after CPU spikes with many long-polling workers over a large backlog. It moved to buffered writes flushed every 10 ms and partitioned events ([Show HN: Hatchet v1](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=43572733)). The only independent multi-system benchmark ran eight Postgres queues on Postgres 18.6. Under sustained pressure, vendor peaks fell to 10–40K/s, several systems timed out, and **append-only designs resisted bloat better than row-mutating ones** ([hardbyte](https://github.com/hardbyte/postgresql-job-queue-benchmarking)). Latency follows the same physics. A library engine pays one commit per step, which DBOS puts at 1–2 ms ([DBOS docs](https://docs.dbos.dev/explanations/comparing-temporal)). Temporal's Eager Workflow Start cut start p50 from 29.3 ms to 16.7 ms by skipping a queue hop ([Temporal](https://docs.temporal.io/design-patterns/eager-workflow-start)). No standard durable-execution benchmark exists; each vendor runs its own harness on incomparable shapes.

## Harvest leads on assurance, trails on throughput, and is thin on agents

Harvest is a 1.06M-line embedded Rust library. Its primary runtime keeps Postgres as the only source of truth. A separate single-writer SQLite backend reuses the same replay engine for edge and local-first use, with no Postgres dependency (`docs/sqlite-backend.md:1-46`). It uses Temporal-style event-sourced replay with hash-routed shards, sticky routing on by default, and since 2026-10-03 resident in-memory workflow state for warm decisions (`docs/changelog.d/pr-1892-resident-workflow-state.md`). Its programming surface is close to Temporal parity. It also has primitives that Temporal lacks as first-class features: transactional activities and starts that commit a domain write with the history event, a durable mutex, business-day timers, debounce and throttle, and completion triggers (`docs/transactional-activities.md`; `docs/completion-triggers.md`). Its stated non-goals are firm. ADR 0002 makes authoring Rust-only (`docs/adr/0002-rust-native-execution-boundary.md`). There is no managed cloud, no hostile multi-tenancy or namespaces (`docs/adr/0004-tenant-isolation-cells.md:83-90`), no native OTLP, no Temporal history import, and no automatic regional failover (`docs/cross-region-dr.md:1-9`).

Several items the research calls open problems are partly built in Harvest already. The literature asks for patch-lifecycle tooling. Harvest's `version-usage --guard` refuses to let a version branch be deleted while executions still record it (`docs/runbooks/version-gate-retirement.md`). The literature finds no sound determinism checker for any language. Harvest layers compile-fail guardrails HVG001–011, `det-check`, and `harvest-verify`. The last is a prototype MIR taint analysis from non-deterministic sources to command-emitting sinks (`docs/harvest-verify.md:1-40`). The literature finds no public model of a Temporal-style engine. Harvest has TLA+ models of activity claims, workflow-task claims and codec rotation, plus a Resonate-style simulation at **4,000,000 seeds a night** (`formal/tla/models.txt`; `docs/adr/0004-deterministic-simulation-testing.md:22-58`). The engine survey found no vendor advertising in-place key rotation or PII erasure of history. Harvest ships both, with a CAS sweep proven replay-identical. That comparison is unverified beyond the vendors sampled.

The weaknesses are equally specific. Harvest's one cross-engine benchmark is a pre-registered negative result. At 2,000 rows of backlog, Harvest's default Postgres mode sustained **5.47 workflows/s against Temporal's 43.29, a 7.91x loss**. The post-hoc depth sweep shows the margin against Harvest's best mode (Redis dispatch) at **roughly 1.5–2x at every depth from 250 to 2,000**. The assay attributes the collapse in plain Postgres mode to one known defect: a non-indexable `ORDER BY` that forces a scan-and-sort on every claim (`docs/assays/0011-harvest-vs-temporal-single-box.md`). Claim throughput drops to **3 claims/s at a 100K backlog** (`docs/performance.md:196-204`). The assay predates post-commit NOTIFY (#1796), deterministic suspension readiness (#1797) and resident state (#1798), and **no post-fix rerun exists**. The current gap is unknown, not closed.

The resident path covers only cycles with exactly one awaited command, no race, no held mutex and no hot-swap. How often real workflows hit it is unmeasured. DST and TLA+ cover the claim protocols and codec rotation only. DST runs an oracle store, not `worker.rs`, and does not model the sweeper, timers or scheduler. Code coverage is unmeasured. Pool-usage, query-latency and poller metrics are still missing (`autumn-harvest/src/telemetry.rs:1518-1534`). The agent layer exists: MCP start/watch/steer tools, best-effort progress streaming, claim-check payload offload, and a Claude agent daemon example. But usage reports count starts and activities, not tokens (`autumn-harvest/src/usage.rs:11-20`). Workflow reset exists as a Temporal-style fork at an event boundary. It **seals the source and always rejects `COMPLETED` and `TERMINATED` sources** (`autumn-harvest/src/reset.rs:273-288`), so it is a recovery tool, not an exploration or evaluation tool. Erased sources are refused only on an opt-in flag. The DAG retry surface sets it. The batch-reset endpoint leaves it off on purpose and can fork an erased `FAILED`, `CANCELLED` or `TIMED_OUT` run (`autumn-harvest/src/reset.rs:826-829`; `autumn-harvest-plugin/src/api.rs:21221-21232`). Most September resilience gaps are now closed, per the capability map's audit of `reports/Autumn Harvest resilience gap analysis.md`. The team's revealed priorities run in this order: correctness and assurance, then safe defaults and operability, then performance. Polyglot reach is deliberately deprioritized.

### Near-term engineering bets reuse machinery Harvest already owns

**The prerequisite: fix the claim path and publish the rerun.** Every pitch Harvest makes runs into a published 7.91x loss. The fix is not research. DBOS's partitioned queue heads gave 2.5x. Hatchet's buffered writes and the append-only advantage in the independent benchmark show the shape of the solution. The claim needs an index-ordered path that no residual predicate can defeat, sharded queue heads, batched claims, and queue state that is inserted rather than updated where possible. Assay 0008's kill of integrated Redis dispatch at 173 tasks/s against a 10,000 target (`docs/assays/0008-redis-dispatch-integrated-throughput.md`) argues for fixing Postgres instead of leaning on Redis. The team should add the missing pool, query and poller gauges and then rerun assay 0011 at every depth. Its pre-registered method can also grow into the open durable-execution benchmark that the field lacks. Such a benchmark would fix step count, payload, durability level, run length long enough to expose bloat, and crash-recovery time. Differentiation is low, but nothing below lands with users while the only comparative number reads 7.91x.

**Rank 1: per-run upgrade-compatibility verdicts.** This is the biggest unsolved user pain, and Harvest holds most of the parts. Pinning already exists through build routing with percent ramps. The patch-retirement guard exists. The replay drift gate samples live histories. The proposal joins these into one pre-deploy check that returns a **migrate, review or pin verdict for every in-flight run**. The check addresses all three Maraschi–Collina failure modes, but its verdicts are conservative, not exact. Bounded replay of each run's recorded prefix against the new build catches determinism violations. Deserializing every recorded payload into the new build's Rust types catches rehydration failure. Today's JSON Schemas are optional and cover only workflow input, output and error (`autumn-harvest/src/info.rs:547-557`), so the check needs type information for activity, signal and side-effect payloads too. A diff of the command-emitting call graph that `harvest-verify` already resolves flags structural drift in steps the run has not reached. It cannot see value-level drift: a changed threshold or activity argument keeps the call graph and passes prefix replay, yet changes the run's future behavior. `harvest-verify` performs taint analysis only, not semantic equivalence (`docs/harvest-verify.md:30-40`). So any change to workflow code reachable from a run's current position must yield "review", not "migrate", unless a stronger equivalence check is added. Harvest has a structural advantage on the privacy blocker. It owns the AEAD codec and KMS integration, so the replay can run inside the operator's trust boundary on decrypted payloads. Production histories need no export or scrubbing. The payoff is **safe auto-upgrade of runs whose reachable workflow code is unchanged**, for example when a deploy changes only activities or steps a run has already passed. Every other run gets a review or pin verdict. No surveyed engine offers that. The risk is that behavioral drift is undecidable in general, so verdicts must default to "review" whenever the analysis meets an `unknown` boundary, which `harvest-verify` already models.

**Rank 2: an agent cost ledger.** Agent workloads drive the market's growth, and no engine meters them where the cost occurs. Harvest should add a typed LLM-call activity, or an `llm` activity attribute, that records model id, input and output tokens, cost and latency as structured completion metadata. That metadata must not ride inside the completion `output`. Under `AeadCodec`, `output` is one of the six encrypted payload fields, and the #1920 clear-text decision covers failure text only (`docs/changelog.d/issue-1920-aead-codec-failure-text-boundary.md:3-5`). SQL usage reports, quota checks and token-aware limits could not aggregate it without decrypting every event. The ledger therefore needs dedicated clear structural columns or a side table keyed by event id. It would then roll up in the existing usage reports. Per-run and per-tenant token or dollar budgets would plug into the quota machinery (#946) and the default-on retry budget (#1793). The gradient-based adaptive concurrency limiter (#1836) can learn provider rate limits keyed on tokens instead of tasks. A **cross-run, content-addressed cache** keyed on model, parameters and a prompt hash, stored through the claim-check `PayloadStore`, would cut the cost of retries, forks and evaluation runs. No source shows any engine caching LLM outputs across runs. The feasibility question is the epic's discipline against new event variants. A side table keyed by event id, or clear columns beside the event row, avoids a variant and keeps replay unchanged. Operators must accept that model ids and token counts are visible without the codec key.

**Rank 3: non-destructive fork and replay-as-evaluation.** `reset.rs` already copies a history prefix into a new execution on the same shard, buffers signals and drains side effects. The extension is a fork mode that leaves the source untouched, accepts completed runs, links the child through lineage, and lets the caller override inputs or recorded activity results at the fork point. On top of that sits an evaluation harness. It re-drives a production history with recorded tool outputs but a live candidate model or prompt, then diffs the decisions. The replay debugger's run diff (#949) and the history-to-test generator already provide the parts. The 2026 rollback-attack and token-replay papers set the guardrail. A fork must **default to recorded or stubbed effects** and require explicit opt-in for live side effects, so it cannot re-run an authorized payment under a new identity. A fork must also reject erased sources unconditionally. Today only the DAG retry surface refuses them, and the batch-reset path does not (`autumn-harvest-plugin/src/api.rs:21221-21232`), so the fork design must not inherit that default. LangGraph has forking over snapshots, and Temporal has reset. A journal engine that markets a fork tree plus regression evaluation over production histories would be first.

**Rank 4: widen assurance to the code paths that changed most recently.** The DST ADR already names the next scope: the timeout sweeper, the FAILED state, quarantine, workflow tasks, the scheduler fire claim and timers. The newest and riskiest path is missing from that list: resident state, which skips replay for warm decisions. Two moves would raise the bar to Resonate's level and past it. The first runs the real worker loop under a seeded simulator, as S2 did in Rust with libc-level clock and entropy overrides that exposed 17 bugs before production ([S2](https://s2.dev/blog/dst)). Today only the oracle store runs. The second uses `harvest_events` histories as traces and validates them against the TLA+ specs in CI. Adding coverage measurement closes the last open item from the September audit. A public, Jepsen-style safety report on lease, fencing, exactly-once completion and signal ordering would be novel for any workflow engine. It would also give the assurance work external credibility that internal CI cannot.

**Rank 5: MCP Tasks done right.** The mapping is almost one-to-one. Task id is execution id, `input_required` is an update or signal wait, `cancelled` is cancellation, and TTL is retention. Harvest already generates start, watch and steer MCP tools. Request-scoped start idempotency (#808) supplies crash-safe idempotent task creation, which the spec needs and does not define. The outbound side is just as close. A workflow that calls a remote MCP or A2A task can journal the task handle as an external task token (`external_task.rs`) and suspend until poll or push resolves it. Two details matter. A tool result with `isError: true` is still `completed`, so it must not trigger activity retries. Debounced and batched workflows have no execution id and remain excluded, as they are from today's MCP tools. The bet is small and well-timed: client support is still arriving, and no rival ships a first-party adapter.

**Ranks 11 and 12: cheaper replay and cheaper history.** Before generalizing resident state to races, fan-out and mutexes, Harvest should measure its hit rate. Sequential agent loops (model call, then tool call, then model call) are largely single-await cycles. The narrow path may already cover the dominant agent shape, and parallel tool calls would show where to extend it. On storage, partitioned events and the `HistoryArchiver` hook can export an aged partition to object storage instead of dropping it. Temporal Cloud's roughly 40x price gap between active and retained storage ([Temporal pricing](https://temporal.io/pricing)) shows the economics. Delta-encoding repeated context windows inside agent histories would attack the 50 MiB default cap at its source. Both are solid engineering work. Neither sets Harvest apart.

### Longer-horizon research bets exploit the embedded-Postgres position

**Rank 6: make workflows statically analyzable.** The `#[workflow]` macro and the MIR verifier together could emit each workflow's step, signal, update and compensation graph as data. That one artifact feeds four uses. It drives the drift diff in Rank 1, and it enables model checking of user workflows: does every completed forward step in a `Saga` have a compensation on every failure path, and are signal handlers free of races with the main body? It also supports a seeded fault-injection simulator for *user* workflows, which no vendor ships even though engines themselves are now routinely tested this way. The deeper move is capability-typed determinism. The workflow context would be the only source of time, randomness and I/O, which would turn Harvest's denylist guardrails into an allowlist guarantee. No Rust crate or other engine claims this. Soundness across async state machines, generics and third-party crates is the hard research problem. `harvest-verify`'s explicit `unknown` boundaries are the right way to stay honest about it.

**Rank 7: speculative durable execution.** libDSE's up-to-10x latency claim is the largest reported gain in the literature. Harvest's architecture fits it unusually well. Every durable fact lands in one Postgres commit, so a speculation boundary is simply "the commit has not happened yet". Effects that reach the outside world (activity dispatch, callbacks, NOTIFY) are already gated behind commit by post-commit NOTIFY and the transactional outbox. A worker could run the next decision optimistically on the resident state while the previous commit flushes, and discard it if the commit or the claim-epoch fence fails. Halfmoon's asymmetric logging attacks the same cost from the write side, and writes per step are the main cost lever for Postgres engines. The risks are serious: interaction with fencing, replay determinism of speculative branches, and repair semantics. This is a research bet, and it should first run inside the DST harness.

**Rank 8: contention-adaptive atomicity.** Harvest already commits a domain write atomically with its history event. That is the precondition for CIDR 2026's per-step physical backout, which runs a short workflow in one transaction with a savepoint per step. The paper's own numbers favour backout under low contention and sagas under long steps or hot items. An engine that **picks the mechanism per workflow from observed contention**, and offers escrow for commutative counters and formally stated saga isolation levels, would ship what the paper lists as future work. The scope is narrow: workflows whose effects live in the same Postgres. That makes it a deep differentiator for the embedded use case rather than a general feature.

**Rank 9: snapshots that decouple upgrades from determinism.** If a run resumes from a typed state snapshot instead of full replay, code changes before the snapshot point stop needing to be deterministic. That would ease versioning, history caps and agent recovery together, and the AstronOS result points the same way for agents. Rust cannot serialize arbitrary futures, so this needs explicit state types and Golem-style save and load functions, with machine-checked preservation of completed side effects. That makes it long-horizon, and it should follow the resident-state hit-rate data.

**Rank 10: a sandboxed agent-code step.** `wasm_activities` already runs signed modules on wasmtime with fuel, epoch and memory limits and a deny-all host surface. Adding host-call journaling and capability grants would give LLM-generated code Golem's main benefit inside a conventional engine. The step stays an *activity*, so it respects ADR 0002's Rust-only authoring boundary. Hosting WASM *workflows* (tier T2) should wait for demonstrated demand, given the spike's "conditional go" verdict (`docs/rnd/hot-code-swap.md:1006-1031`).

**Rank 13: new programming abstractions.** Durable promises, keyed virtual objects and Nexus-style cross-service calls are real gaps, but Restate, Resonate and Temporal already own them. External task tokens are a natural seed for promises. Cross-service calls collide with the no-cross-shard-transactions design, so this belongs behind customer pull, not ahead of it.

### What to decline, and one cheap standards play

The research reinforces the stated non-goals. Polyglot worker SDKs contradict ADR 0002, and `docs/comparison.md:89` still lists a TypeScript worker SDK as planned; that drift should be corrected. Active-active multi-region is not where even Temporal competes: its offering is active/standby with sub-minute RPO and a 20-minute RTO ([Temporal RPO/RTO](https://docs.temporal.io/cloud/rpo-rto)). Harvest's fenced, operator-initiated DR is a reasonable answer for a self-hosted library. Redis Cluster dispatch is the wrong lever after assay 0008. Declining native OTLP leaves a better opening. OpenTelemetry has **no official workflow semantic conventions**, and Dapr only began codifying its own with OTel Weaver in 2026 ([OpenTelemetry blog](https://opentelemetry.io/blog/2026/dapr-workflow-observability/)). ADR 0001's eight span kinds and `harvest.*` attributes, including a rule against duplicate spans during replay, could become a cross-vendor proposal at little cost.

## Conclusion

The central finding is a mismatch. The industry's hardest open problems are the ones a verification stack solves: proving an upgrade safe, giving resume and fork precise semantics, and accounting for non-deterministic, expensive agent steps. Yet the engines with the most users have the least verification machinery, while Harvest has the most and markets it as internal hygiene. The strategic move is to **expose correctness as features users can see and run**: a compatibility verdict before every deploy, a fork that cannot repeat an authorized effect, and a cost ledger that the replay engine already knows how to keep deterministic. Raw throughput will not win Harvest the market. Temporal's scale and capital make that race unwinnable for a single-team library, and the Temporal Rust SDK and DBOS Rust SDK erode the "Rust plus Postgres" niche by themselves.

The order of operations matters as much as the ranking. The claim-path fix and a published post-fix benchmark come first, because a credible number buys the right to be heard. Instrumentation comes next: the resident-path hit rate and the token metadata are cheap to collect, and they decide whether Ranks 9 and 11 are worth their risk. The deepest long-run asset is the one no competitor can copy quickly. That asset is a co-located Postgres commit point, which makes speculation boundaries, physical backout and exact compatibility checks tractable. Each long-horizon bet should therefore enter through the DST harness before it enters the engine. That keeps Harvest's revealed priority, correctness first, intact while it reaches for performance and agent features.
