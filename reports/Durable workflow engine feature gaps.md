# Harvest wins on depth, loses on reach

Autumn Harvest 0.7.0 (HEAD `301ea95`) is no longer short of workflow primitives. It implements almost every Temporal programming-model feature, and it adds Inngest-style flow control, DBOS-style transactional steps and a deeper determinism and verification toolchain than any peer surveyed. Its real gaps sit at the edges of the engine, not in the core. Ranked by impact, the unintended gaps are these. **Measured throughput trails Temporal at every backlog depth tested** on the same box and the same Postgres, by the project's own pre-registered assay. The gap is roughly 1.5x to 2x against Harvest's best mode, and up to 7.91x against its default Postgres claim path at deep backlogs. There is **no agent-integration layer**: no framework adapters, no durable resumable streams, and no keyed entity or session primitive. **Multi-tenancy is thin**, with no namespaces, no fairness keys and no per-tenant authorization. **Enterprise security is delegated**: no OIDC or SSO, three coarse roles, and encryption that leaves execution columns and errors in clear. The operator UI is less mature than its peers'. First-party S3/GCS storage and archival adapters are missing, and AWS is the only KMS provider. Failover is manual only. Several other gaps are deliberate and documented: no polyglot SDKs (ADR 0002), no managed cloud, no serverless push workers and no pluggable persistence. These choices place Harvest in the DBOS-style "library on your own Postgres" category, which the market now validates. They also shut it out of the fastest-growing 2026 segments: serverless durable functions, polyglot agent stacks and managed control planes. The earlier resilience report (HEAD `937b655`) is largely superseded, because spot checks at HEAD find its main P0 defects fixed. The repository's own comparison and agent-facing docs, however, now understate the code in some places and overstate the competitive position in others.

## Harvest covers the Temporal surface and adds what Temporal leaves out

Measured against the 2026 reference set, Harvest's programming model is complete. Workflows are `#[workflow]` async functions, and activities, local activities, side effects and deterministic time, UUID and random all exist (`context.rs:5074-5491`, `context.rs:8298`). Signals come in pull and push forms, typed queries exist, and updates have validators. Signal-with-start and update-with-start are exposed in process and over HTTP (`context.rs:8825-13513`; `autumn-harvest-plugin/src/api.rs:4847-4924`). This matches Temporal's three message types ([Temporal message passing](https://docs.temporal.io/encyclopedia/workflow-message-passing)). Harvest goes further with idempotency-keyed signal delivery. Child workflows support all three parent-close policies, detached children and cross-shard placement. Continue-as-new works across workflow types. Reset accepts three reset-point kinds plus batch reset, and a `Saga` helper and task-token async completion are built in (`types.rs:1234-1250`; `reset.rs:80-90`; `saga.rs:119-276`; `context.rs:12398-12422`). Versioning has `version`/`patched`/`deprecate_patch` plus build-ID routing with a percentage ramp (`context.rs:5512-5712`; `build_routing.rs:1-6`). That is the same pin-plus-patch combination Temporal made GA at Replay 2026 ([Replay 2026](https://temporal.io/blog/replay-2026-product-announcements)). Schedules lack only Temporal's `AllowAll` overlap mode. Otherwise they offer cron with IANA zones, catch-up, backfill, jitter, pause and holiday calendars (`policy.rs:731-995`; `calendar.rs:1-13`).

Harvest exceeds the reference engine in four areas. The first is **flow control**. Debounce, throttle, event batching, per-key concurrency with latest-wins, four-level priority, weighted queue fairness, tenant quotas, an adaptive concurrency limit, retry budgets and circuit breakers are all declarative attributes or admin settings (`debounce.rs`, `throttle.rs`, `event_batch.rs`, `concurrency.rs`, `quota.rs`, `adaptive_limit.rs`, `retry_budget.rs`). That set is closer to Inngest's ([Inngest flow control](https://www.inngest.com/docs/guides/flow-control.md)) than to Temporal's. It is also broader than Restate 1.7's flow control, which made concurrency limits GA but still lists rate limits and priorities only on its roadmap ([Restate 1.7](https://restate.dev/blog/announcing-restate-1-7); [Restate roadmap](https://docs.restate.dev/roadmap/oss.md)). The second is **operator brakes**: per-execution pause and resume, queue and activity-type pause, admission gates and backlog-age load shedding (`queue_pause.rs`, `activity_pause.rs`, `admission_gate.rs`, `load_shed.rs`). Temporal's workflow pause is still pre-release ([Workflow Pause](https://docs.temporal.io/encyclopedia/workflow/workflow-pause)). The third is **determinism assurance**. Compile-time guardrails HVG001-HVG011, the `det_check` analyzer, a MIR-level taint verifier, replay canaries and drift gates are all present. Non-deterministic runs are parked rather than failed. TLA+ models, Kani proofs, fuzzing and seeded deterministic simulation round out the set (`docs/comparison.md:111`; `autumn-harvest-verify/README.md`; `formal/tla/`; `dst/mod.rs`). Determinism is the most-cited Temporal pain point ([Vanlightly, Nov 2025](https://jack-vanlightly.com/blog/2025/11/24/demystifying-determinism-in-durable-execution); [ZenML, Dec 2025](https://www.zenml.io/blog/temporal-alternatives)), so this tooling answers a real complaint. The fourth is **transactional coupling with application data**. `run_transactional` commits a domain write atomically with `ActivityCompleted`, and `start_workflow_transactional` gives a transactional outbox start (`context.rs:15778`; `handle.rs:762`). The CIDR 2026 "AC/DC" paper builds its case on exactly this step-plus-business-write primitive ([Stonebraker et al., CIDR 2026](https://www.vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p9-stonebraker.pdf)).

The data-lifecycle and operations surface is also strong for a library. Default history caps are 50,000 events and 50 MiB, close to Temporal's 51,200 events and 50 MB ([Temporal defaults](https://docs.temporal.io/self-hosted-guide/defaults)). A claim-check `PayloadStore` hook exists (`payload_store.rs`). Harvest also ships an AES-256-GCM codec with in-place key rotation, PII erasure, legal hold, shard-pinned data residency, an audit trail with SIEM export and an optional hash chain, and opt-in retention with partition drops. Its N/N-1 rolling-upgrade contract is enforced by a CI lock-safety lint (`docs/upgrading/README.md:1-75`). About 80 bounded-cardinality metrics, an SLO burn-rate alert pack, 24 runbooks, a synthetic canary and a CLI with nearly 40 top-level commands complete the operator kit (`docs/telemetry.md`; `docs/alerts/slo.md`; `autumn-harvest-cli/src/lib.rs:1119-1517`). Postgres-engine users raise two recurring complaints: queue-table bloat, and "just use a database" growing into a home-made engine ([HN 48313530](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48313530)). Harvest already answers both, with partitioned events, a seven-day terminal-row janitor (#1811) and the breadth above.

| Area | Harvest at `301ea95` | Strongest peer reference |
|---|---|---|
| Message passing, children, continue-as-new, reset | Parity, plus keyed signal dedupe | Temporal |
| Flow control (debounce, throttle, batch, quotas, priority) | **Exceeds Temporal**; near Inngest | Inngest, Hatchet, DBOS queues |
| Operator brakes (pause, gates, shedding, breakers) | **Exceeds** most peers | Temporal (pause pre-release), Restate 1.6 pause |

## Deliberate non-goals trade the fastest-growing segments for focus

Five absences are design decisions, not oversights, and an evaluator should read them that way. **ADR 0002** states that Harvest "will not maintain official polyglot worker runtimes, language SDKs, or a remote activity worker protocol unless this ADR is superseded". It routes non-Rust code through task-token external activities, signals, webhooks and the management API instead (`docs/adr/0002-rust-native-execution-boundary.md:44-60`). **No managed cloud** is stated as "a deliberate positioning choice, not a backlog gap" (`docs/comparison.md:166`). The project also declines **pluggable persistence**: "harvest cannot swap in Cassandra" (`docs/comparison.md:272-276`). **Automatic cross-region failover** is likewise out of scope. Issue #954 ships a write-authority fence, an RPO metric and a runbook, but "no automatic promotion, no active-active writing, and no zero-RPO mode" (`docs/cross-region-dr.md:1-35`). Several smaller items are documented design substitutions: server-side decode-on-read replaces a codec server (`docs/operations/read-path-decode.md`), and a Collector recipe replaces native OTLP (`docs/adr/0004-security-extras.md:156-175`). Tenant cells "bound load, not access" (`docs/security-posture.md:684-697`). Cross-shard global limits are out of scope (`docs/sharding.md:401`). WASM activities and hot code swap are labelled R&D spikes, not GA commitments (`hot_swap.rs:1-6`).

These choices cost more in October 2026 than when ADR 0002 was accepted in May 2026, for three reasons. First, **Rust is no longer an uncontested niche**. Temporal's Rust SDK went GA on 4 September 2026, making eight official Temporal SDKs ([Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log); [Temporal SDKs](https://docs.temporal.io/encyclopedia/temporal-sdks)). Restate ships a Rust SDK with virtual objects ([restate-sdk](https://docs.rs/restate-sdk)). Embedded Rust crates now cover the same pitch, including `durare` (DBOS-compatible) and `durable-workflows` (Diesel on Postgres or MySQL) ([durare](https://github.com/SamuelXing/durare); [durable-workflows](https://github.com/steventhanna/durable-workflows)). A Rust team choosing Harvest over Temporal now trades away polyglot reach and a managed cloud for an embedded deployment, where before it gained a Rust-native SDK. Second, **push-based serverless execution became mainstream**. AWS Lambda durable functions run up to a year with zero-compute waits ([AWS Lambda durable functions](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lambda/latest/dg/durable-functions.html)). Temporal serverless workers reached public preview on Lambda on 3 August 2026 ([Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log)). Restate, Inngest, Vercel and Cloudflare all invoke handlers over HTTP ([Restate 1.7](https://restate.dev/blog/announcing-restate-1-7); [Vercel Workflows](https://vercel.com/docs/workflow)). Harvest's in-process long-lived pollers cannot follow, and ADR 0002's ban on a remote worker protocol blocks the obvious bridge. Third, **managed control planes now come with the open-source library**. DBOS pairs its Postgres library with Conductor at $99 per month per extra executor ([DBOS pricing](https://dbos.dev/dbos-pricing)). Temporal added a pay-as-you-go tier with a $0 support minimum on 15 September 2026 ([Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log)).

The case for the stance is strong, though. The embedded-library-on-Postgres model is a recognised and growing category: DBOS needs "no infrastructure required besides Postgres" ([DBOS architecture](https://docs.dbos.dev/architecture)), and Postgres-only alternatives keep drawing Hacker News attention because self-hosted Temporal is widely seen as heavy ([HN 48313530](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48313530)). A single-language engine can also enforce determinism at compile time, which no polyglot SDK family does. The cost is that the TypeScript management client, shipped in 0.7.0 as a GitHub release tarball and not on npm, is the only non-Rust artifact (`clients/typescript/README.md:8-18`). No Python client exists. Non-Rust teams can call Harvest, but they cannot author for it.

**Throughput is the top gap because it undercuts the pitch for the whole category.** Harvest's own pre-registered assay #11 ran Harvest and Temporal's Go server against the same PostgreSQL 16 instance on four cores. The registered verdict was "KILL on L1, decisively and against harvest, by 7.91x" (`docs/assays/0011-harvest-vs-temporal-single-box.md:1, 53-56, 116-136`). That figure needs scoping. The assay's post-hoc depth sweep shows Temporal ahead at every depth from 250 to 2,000, but by roughly 1.5x to 2x against Harvest's best mode (Redis dispatch). The 7.91x figure is the default Postgres mode at the depth where its claim path is worst. The assay attributes that collapse to the known `#786`/`#1177` scan-and-sort claim query, and it states that one shape on one box does not license a general performance claim (`docs/assays/0011-harvest-vs-temporal-single-box.md:155-224`). The published headline is 23.73 workflows per second on one shard for v0.6.0, and no v0.7.0 figure exists (`docs/benchmarks.md:37-50`). Buyers move to Postgres-only engines because Temporal is "ridiculously heavy infra wise" ([HN 48313530](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48313530)). An embedded engine that runs slower than Temporal on Temporal's own database loses that argument. DBOS claims 43,000 workflows per second on one Postgres server from in-memory concurrency tracking, READ COMMITTED isolation and partial indexes ([DBOS, May 2026](https://dbos.dev/blog/new-in-dbos-may-2026)). That figure is vendor-reported and uses a different workload, so it is no head-to-head result, but it shows the ceiling buyers will cite. Two caveats apply. The assay was pre-registered on 16 September 2026, so it may predate the resident-state cache (#1798) and adaptive limits (#1836). And the honest pre-registration is itself a governance strength. The finding that matters is narrower than the headline ratio. Harvest's default mode collapses under backlog through one documented, fixable claim-path defect. Even its best mode still trails Temporal by a margin that no doc reconciles with the lightweight-engine pitch.

**The agent layer is the second gap, because the demand has moved there.** Temporal's $5B February 2026 round was tied to agent workloads ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/temporal-raises-300m-hits-5b-valuation-as-seattle-infrastructure-startup-rides-ai-wave/)). Every managed vendor now ships an agent primitive: Step Functions' AgentCore reasoning step, Azure durable agents, Conductor's LLM and MCP system tasks, and Camunda 8.8's AI Agent connector ([Step Functions launches](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/step-functions/latest/dg/recent-launches.html); [Azure durable agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/durable-task/sdks/durable-agents-microsoft-agent-framework); [Orkes AI tasks](https://docs.orkes.io/content/category/reference-docs/ai-tasks)). The pattern has converged. The agent loop is the workflow, model and tool calls are steps, the session is an entity, and approval is a durable wait. Harvest has three of these four pieces as primitives. It has no session entity, and it ships no adapter that wires a framework to them. Its MCP tool exposure (`#[workflow(mcp)]`) and the Claude agent daemon example are real assets (`docs/mcp-tools.md`; `examples/claude-agent-daemon/README.md`), but they need autumn-web and are not offered as a library. The cheapest fix is visible in the market. Pydantic AI now offers a "durable execution backend builder" for third-party engines ([Pydantic AI](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/capabilities/durable_execution/overview/)), and Restate integrates through Pydantic AI's public interface alone ([Pydantic AI overview](https://pydantic.dev/docs/ai/integrations/durable_execution/overview/index.md)). Those are Python routes that ADR 0002 forbids, though. A Rust-native adapter for an agent crate is the in-policy option. A durable, resumable stream comes next. `publish_progress` is never recorded, and chunks "can drop under back-pressure" (`docs/streaming-progress.md:96`), whereas Vercel streams let clients disconnect and resume at any point ([Workflow SDK AI](https://workflow-sdk.dev/docs/ai)). The append-only event log could carry stream chunks if the history caps allow it.

**Keyed state and tenancy follow.** Restate's virtual objects ([Restate building blocks](https://docs.restate.dev/concepts/durable_building_blocks)) and Azure's durable entities, which Microsoft uses for each agent session ([Azure durable agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/durable-task/sdks/durable-agents-microsoft-agent-framework)), have no Harvest equivalent. Users must combine a deterministic workflow ID, signal-with-start, `concurrency(limit = 1)` and queries to fake one. Vanlightly names "sessions and actors" as two of the three durable-function forms ([Vanlightly blog](https://jack-vanlightly.com/blog)), so this gap is about the programming model, not convenience. On tenancy, Temporal made weighted fairness keys GA on 5 May 2026, with per-key rate limits and up to 1,000 weight overrides ([Temporal priority and fairness](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/task-queue-priority-fairness)). DBOS partitions queues by tenant ([DBOS queues](https://docs.dbos.dev/typescript/reference/transactapi/workflow-queues)), and Hatchet offers group round robin with CEL keys ([Hatchet concurrency](https://docs.hatchet.run/home/concurrency)). Harvest weights queues, not keys, and its key limits are shard-local. Harvest's own security doc states that it "has no namespaces" and no per-tenant row scoping (`docs/security-posture.md:679-704`). That suits one operator serving internal tenants. It falls short for a SaaS embedder whose customers are mutually hostile.

**The remaining gaps are integration depth, not engine design.** Security is "safe if configured". Tokens, rate limiting, the hash chain, the codec and the authorizer are all opt-in, and authentication belongs to the host app (`docs/security-posture.md:7-12, 513-545`). Fail-closed mutations is the main secure default. Enterprise buyers expect RBAC with custom roles and SSO, which Orkes and Temporal Cloud ship ([Conductor OSS vs Orkes](https://docs.orkes.io/platform/conductor-oss-vs-orkes); [Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log)). The codec leaves execution input, output, memo, search attributes, signals, DLQ input and every error string in clear (`docs/security-posture.md:878-929`), and only AWS KMS is supported. Claim-check offload and archival are hooks with no first-party S3 or GCS implementation. Temporal ships both drivers, applies the codec before upload, and pitches the feature for agent conversation histories ([Temporal External Storage](https://docs.temporal.io/external-storage)). Broker ingress covers Kafka and SQS only. There is no HTTP or LLM "system task". Step Functions' 220-plus integrations ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/lambda/latest/dg/durable-step-functions.html)) and Camunda's connector marketplace set an expectation that a code-first library meets only through user-written activities. That is acceptable for Rust developers, but it becomes a gap when the buyer is a platform team.

Four smaller items round out the list. The first is the missing primitives. There is no payload-matching event wait like Cloudflare's `step.waitForEvent` ([Cloudflare Workflows](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workflows/)), no cancellation scopes, no first-class durable promise like Restate's awakeables ([Restate awakeables](https://docs.restate.dev/develop/ts/awakeables)), no counting semaphore and no `AllowAll` overlap mode. The second is fan-out ergonomics: Harvest has windowed fan-out helpers (`context.rs:10362-10933`) but no analogue to Step Functions' Distributed Map tolerated-failure threshold or S3 result writer ([Distributed Map](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/step-functions/latest/dg/state-map-distributed.html)). The third is the missing standalone durable-job primitive. Temporal made Standalone Activities GA on 15 September 2026 ([Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log)), and the notes found no equivalent beyond the Redis dispatch crate's task queue. The fourth is packaging. There is no container image, Helm chart or operator, and `release.yml` has no `cargo publish` step, even though the README shows a crates.io badge. The project is 0.x and has no LTS window (`README.md:1122-1123`).

## Repair tooling and versioning meet the bar the market set in 2026

Two areas that comparison articles flag as differentiators are covered, and they deserve explicit credit. The first is **fixing forward from a failure**. DBOS fork and rewind ([DBOS workflow management](https://docs.dbos.dev/python/tutorials/workflow-management)), Restate's restart from a journal prefix ([Restate changelog](https://docs.restate.dev/changelog/server.md)) and Step Functions' 14-day redrive ([Step Functions quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/step-functions/latest/dg/service-quotas.html)) all have Harvest counterparts. Reset to an event ID, first activity run or last workflow task replays on current code, with signal re-apply control. Harvest also offers preview, batch reset, rerun, DAG retry-from-failed-node, and single-activity retry-now and fail-now (`reset.rs:29-1074`; `docs/openapi.json`). Harvest has no "copy to a new workflow ID" fork. That is a small difference, unless an idempotency key forces a new identity. The second is **versioning in-flight runs**, which the market calls the most durable unsolved pain point ([HN 44840693](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44840693)). Build-ID routing pins tasks to compatible workers and ramps by percentage. Workflow-type reachability, version-gate usage and retirement checks, and a payload schema gate sit on top (`build_routing.rs`; `version_gate_retirement.rs`; `harvest schema check`). Azure's instance-pinned version with `Strict`/`CurrentOrOlder` match strategies ([Azure orchestration versioning](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-functions/durable/durable-functions-orchestration-versioning)) is no stronger.

One market signal deserves attention because it cuts against Harvest's core design. Several Rust crates market "no deterministic replay" as a feature. Sayiir checkpoints "after each task ... no deterministic replay" ([Sayiir](https://github.com/sayiir)). Absurd allows non-deterministic code outside steps ([Ronacher, Apr 2026](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/4/4/absurd-in-production.md)), and Trigger.dev and Golem escape the replay model entirely ([indepth.dev](https://indepth.dev/posts/1020/en/how-trigger-dev-checkpoints-containers); [Golem](https://learn.golem.cloud/why-golem)). One hypothesis is that idiomatic async Rust resists replay constraints. The notes found no primary Rust-user evidence for it. Harvest's answer is to make replay safe with compile-time checks, not to avoid it. That is defensible, but the docs should argue for it explicitly, because an evaluator comparing Harvest with a checkpoint-only crate will otherwise see only the constraint.

The earlier resilience report at `937b655` should no longer guide prioritisation. Spot checks at HEAD show that its P0 and P1 items were addressed in code. Retry jitter now defaults to `Full` (`policy.rs:169-195`). The Postgres claim path holds a `DispatchReservation` so it no longer over-claims past free permits (#1312, `worker.rs:30666-30690`). Per-role pool and session timeouts exist (`pool.rs:144-169`). Activity completion and failure writes go through a `fence(update, claim)` filter on worker ID and attempt (`queue.rs:2865-2870`). The production `drive_workflow` path now treats a park that skips a recorded command as drift (#1791). The 100 ms `SUSPENSION_TIMEOUT` constant is gone from `executor.rs`. `chaos.yml` parses and has a nightly schedule. A database trigger now enforces the append-only invariant (#1817), and default history caps, Kubernetes probes, burn-rate alerts and TLA+ models all landed. These are grep-level checks, not test runs. One caveat remains: the public `queue::complete_task` still passes no claim (`queue.rs:3610-3619`), so any caller that uses it bypasses the fence. That path should be audited.

## Repository docs lag the code in at least ten places

Evaluators read docs, not source, and Harvest's docs misstate its position in both directions. `docs/comparison.md` dates its competitor facts to 2026-07-14 (`docs/comparison.md:17`). Since then, Temporal's Rust SDK went GA, Temporal serverless workers entered preview, Standalone Activities went GA, and DBOS Go reached 1.0 ([Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log); [DBOS, Aug 2026](https://dbos.dev/blog/new-in-dbos-august-2026)). The page's competitive claims are therefore now too favourable to Harvest.

## Conclusion

Harvest's competitive question has changed. It is no longer whether a Rust-embedded engine can reach Temporal's feature surface. Harvest has reached it, and in flow control, operator brakes and determinism assurance it has passed Temporal. The open question now is efficiency and reach. A library sold as the lightweight alternative must beat the heavyweight on the heavyweight's own database, and assay #11 says it does not yet, at that one shape. Fixing the default Postgres claim path (`#786`/`#1177`) closes the dramatic part of the gap. The remaining 1.5x to 2x against the best mode is the harder question, and a broader benchmark should settle it before any new primitive.

The second implication concerns strategy. ADR 0002 and the no-cloud stance are coherent, but they now bind against the market's main growth vector, durable agents. Inside the policy, the useful moves are to ship an entity or session primitive, a durable resumable stream, and a Rust-native adapter for an agent framework, all of which the event log can already support. Paired with first-party S3 offload and tenant fairness keys, those would make Harvest the strongest choice for its stated buyer. That buyer is a Rust team that wants agent workloads, tenant isolation and transactional coupling on the Postgres it already runs. Until the comparison page, the skill file and the license text catch up, though, that buyer will judge Harvest by the docs, and the docs currently describe a different product.
